Statistical Analysis of Stop and Search Practices on Black Communities in the UK

Author

Michel Mesquita, Ph.D., CStat

Published

2026-07-28

This report presents the final findings of the UNJUST project, which examines the impact of police stop and search powers on Black communities in England and Wales. Conducted as part of the Statisticians for Society programme, the project delivers a comprehensive analysis supported by literature reviews, statistical modelling, and data evaluation. All methodologies and findings are documented and accessible through the GitHub repository accompanying this report (Mesquita, 2023).

0.1 Research Questions

The analysis addresses four research questions identified by UNJUST:

  1. Disproportionate Targeting: Do Black individuals in metropolitan areas experience higher rates of drug-related stop and searches compared to other ethnic groups?
  2. Youth Impact: How do stop and search rates vary for young individuals (under 25 years old) in drug-related cases?
  3. Trend over Time: Has the adjusted disparity in stop and search rates changed over the five-year period 2020/21–2024/25?
  4. Outcome Disparity: Do post-search outcomes differ by ethnicity, suggesting differential thresholds for suspicion?

0.2 Scope and Data

This report covers five complete financial years, 2020/21 through 2024/25, using the GOV.UK Stop and Search Open Data (556,000+ aggregated records representing approximately 2.9 million individual stop-and-search incidents) merged with ONS Census 2021 population denominators.

0.3 Key Findings

  • Raw national disparity: In 2024/25, Black individuals were stopped at 3.6 times the rate of White individuals, the headline figure shown on the accompanying dashboard. This is the unadjusted rate ratio (rounded to 1 d.p.), calculated directly from search counts divided by 2021 Census population denominators, with no covariates controlled.
  • Persistent and growing adjusted disparity: After controlling for region, sex, age, and reason for search, the adjusted incidence rate ratio (IRR) rises from 3× in 2020/21 to 3.3× by 2024/25, a statistically significant worsening. The adjusted figure is lower than the raw 3.6× because the model accounts for differences in regional composition and search type across ethnic groups; the gap between the two measures reflects those compositional differences, not measurement error.
  • Interaction effects: The compounding of ethnicity, search reason, and geography means that a Black individual stopped for drug-related reasons in London is searched at approximately 21.9 times the rate of a White individual stopped for non-drug reasons in the East Midlands, holding sex and age constant.
  • Regional anomaly: The South West has the highest Black/White disparity ratio (5.1×), exceeding London (3.6× pooled across all five years), suggesting nationally uneven patterns of disproportionality.
  • Outcome disparity: Black individuals have one of the highest arrest rates across all ethnic groups (16%, tied with Mixed), 2.7 percentage points above White individuals (13.3%), yet similar No Further Action rates (~69%), raising questions about differential suspicion thresholds.
  • Disparity cuts across all search reasons: The racial gap is not confined to drug stops. Every major reason for search, from weapons to articles for use in crime, shows a Black/White rate ratio substantially above parity, indicating a systemic rather than enforcement-specific pattern (see Section 5.10).
  • Missing ethnicity data is concentrated in a subset of forces: Self-defined ethnicity is voluntary: individuals are not required to state it during a stop, and around 1 in 10 records nationally has no ethnicity recorded. The rate varies substantially across forces, which limits the comparability of force-level disparity estimates (see Section 5.11).

These findings are intended to inform policy reform discussions and contribute to broader conversations on fairness and equity in law enforcement. An interactive dashboard accompanying this report is available at unjust.netlify.app.
