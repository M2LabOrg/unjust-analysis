3  Literature Review

Stop and search practices by the police in the United Kingdom are intended to play a significant role in maintaining public order and safety. These powers, primarily governed by the Police and Criminal Evidence Act 1984 (PACE) (legislation.gov.uk, 1984) and the Criminal Justice and Public Order Act 1994 (CJPOA) (legislation.gov.uk, 1994), enable the police to intervene and prevent potential crimes, particularly in areas with higher levels of criminal activity. However, concerns have been raised regarding the disproportionate use of stop and search on Black and ethnic minority communities. This review synthesizes key findings from recent literature, exploring both the effectiveness of stop and search as a policing tool and the concerns over racial disparities.

Stop and search powers have evolved over time and have been utilized in crime prevention efforts. For instance, data from the Home Office for the year ending March 2023 indicates that 547,002 stop and searches were conducted in England and Wales, marking a 3% increase from the previous year. Of these searches, 14% resulted in arrests, highlighting their role in identifying potential offenders and intervening in criminal behaviour (Home Office (GOV.UK), 2023). However, disparities persist, as Black individuals were 5.5 times more likely to be stopped than White individuals in 2020/21, with this ratio decreasing to 4.8 in 2021/22 and 4.1 in 2022/23. These figures prompt further examination of the factors underlying such disparities (Home Office (GOV.UK), 2023).

Broader context on ethnic disparities across the criminal justice system is provided by the Ministry of Justice’s annual Statistics on Race and the Criminal Justice System (Ministry of Justice, 2023). The most recent edition documents persistent over-representation of Black individuals at every stage of the criminal justice pipeline: from stop and search and arrests through to prosecution, conviction, and custodial sentencing. Notably, Black individuals accounted for approximately 9% of arrests in England and Wales despite comprising around 3.5% of the population, a pattern of disproportionality that closely mirrors the disparities observed in stop and search data. This consistency across multiple points of contact with the criminal justice system reinforces the argument that the disparities measured in stop and search are not statistical artefacts of enforcement discretion alone, but form part of a broader, systemic pattern of unequal treatment (Ministry of Justice, 2023).

The causes of these disparities are multifaceted. Racial disparities in stop and search rates have been consistently observed, with several underlying factors potentially contributing to this trend. These include the demographic concentration of certain ethnic groups in areas with higher crime rates and operational strategies focused on specific high-risk areas. Localized crime trends, patterns of criminal activity occurring within specific geographic areas, often guide such strategies, with increased law enforcement activity targeting regions with higher reported rates of violent crime or drug-related offenses. Additionally, the subjective nature of suspicion during searches adds another layer of complexity. For example, the IER UK report on York highlights that Black individuals in North Yorkshire are 18 times more likely to be stopped than White individuals, partly attributed to over-policing in neighborhoods with higher crime rates (IER UK, 2023).

These observed disparities highlight not only operational challenges in stop and search but also raise critical questions about broader systemic inequalities that influence its application. Addressing these inequalities requires a deeper understanding of their socio-economic and historical roots, as explored in reports like the Runnymede Trust’s The Colour of Money (Runnymede, 2020) and the Equality and Human Rights Commission’s (EHRC) Stop and Think (Equality and Commission, 2010).

Stop and search is one of many tools used by the police to tackle crime, but its effectiveness and broader implications remain contested. The Runnymede Trust’s The Colour of Money report does not directly address policing tactics like stop and search but highlights the systemic racial inequalities that influence socio-economic outcomes and the lived experiences of Black and ethnic minority communities. These systemic factors, including discrimination and socio-economic disparities, may indirectly shape the context in which stop and search is applied and perceived. The report underscores that addressing such inequalities requires systemic changes, suggesting that targeted interventions in high-crime areas may not fully resolve underlying racial disparities without broader structural reforms to tackle economic and social inequities (Runnymede, 2020).

Similarly, the Equality and Human Rights Commission’s (EHRC) report, Stop and Think, critically examines the disproportionate use of stop and search powers (Equality and Commission, 2010). It highlights that while stop and search can provide a means to confirm or allay suspicion without arrest, the evidence suggests it has limited effectiveness in reducing crime rates, contributing only to a 0.2% reduction in disruptable crimes. The report also underscores that disproportionate targeting of Black and Asian individuals undermines trust in the police, negatively affecting community relations. The EHRC emphasizes that any effectiveness of stop and search should be balanced against these societal costs, suggesting that policing strategies must be both fair and proportionate to foster public confidence.

Concerns have been raised about the broader societal impacts of stop and search, particularly regarding its effect on community trust. Repeated law enforcement encounters can erode trust, fostering feelings of alienation and mistrust among minority communities. The use of facial recognition technology adds another layer of complexity (Sylvester, 2024), as advanced surveillance tools may reinforce perceptions of unfair targeting. While intended to enhance policing efficiency, facial recognition technology raises concerns about algorithmic biases that disproportionately misidentify individuals from minority groups, further compounding these perceptions. Such tools, when integrated into stop and search operations, may exacerbate perceptions of bias, particularly if algorithmic inaccuracies disproportionately affect minority groups. Nevertheless, the use of such technologies is arguably framed as a means to enhance data-driven decision-making and refine localized risk assessments to address specific crime trends.

Conversely, police forces emphasize that stop and search is used proportionately and in response to specific intelligence or local crime trends. According to the Commission on Race and Ethnic Disparities, operational decisions related to stop and search are often guided by crime data and localized risk assessments, which aim to address high-risk areas while maintaining fairness and transparency (Commission on Race and Ethnic Disparities, 2021).

The Centre for Social Justice’s Facing the Facts report (2020) highlights the importance of understanding socio-economic disparities both within and across ethnic groups. It challenges the broad use of the “BAME” (Black and Minority Ethnic) category, arguing that granular data, detailed and disaggregated information on individual ethnic subgroups, provides a more nuanced understanding of disparities in areas such as education, employment, and family stability (Social Justice, 2020). These disparities often overlap with patterns of localized crime, influencing both the prevalence of police interventions and public perceptions of fairness in policing practices. By addressing these socio-economic disparities and fostering inclusivity across public services, these strategies aim to reduce systemic drivers of racial disparities in policing and enhance community trust. Such interventions not only address root causes of crime but may also reduce the need for targeted policing strategies that disproportionately affect minority communities. In tandem with policing reforms, addressing broader socio-economic inequalities through targeted interventions in education and employment could reduce the systemic drivers of these disparities.

The Home Office’s report for 2023 indicates that 71% of stop and searches resulted in no further action, raising questions about the criteria for suspicion and the balance between proactive policing and the protection of civil liberties (Home Office (GOV.UK), 2023). The Home Office data underscores the scale of stop and search operations, yet the high proportion of no-action outcomes highlights the challenges of balancing proactive policing with civil liberties. While some may interpret this as a sign of inefficiency, it can also be seen as indicative of the difficulty in balancing public safety with the protection of civil liberties.

In response to these concerns, several recommendations have been proposed. The Commission on Race and Ethnic Disparities advocates for greater transparency and improved community relations, suggesting that community-led policing models and more rigorous oversight could help restore trust between police and minority communities (Commission on Race and Ethnic Disparities, 2021). Additionally, the IER UK’s anti-racism strategy for York emphasizes the importance of anti-racism training for public service professionals, including cultural awareness, unconscious bias, and inclusivity practices. The strategy focuses on fostering improved relations with York’s diverse communities by embedding anti-racism principles across all public services. It advocates for greater community engagement, encouraging active dialogue between public institutions and underrepresented groups to shape policies collaboratively. Furthermore, the strategy highlights the need for accountability through regular monitoring and transparent reporting on racial disparities, ensuring that systemic reforms lead to measurable progress (IER UK, 2023).

In conclusion, while stop and search remains a vital tool for maintaining public safety, evidence highlights its disproportionate impact on Black and ethnic minority communities, warranting careful consideration. These disparities stem from both operational and systemic challenges, including socio-economic inequalities, subjective enforcement criteria, shaped by an officer’s interpretation of ‘reasonable suspicion’, and broader societal impacts on community trust. Subjective enforcement, influenced by implicit biases and contextual judgments, exacerbates these disparities, perpetuating systemic inequalities. Achieving equitable and effective policing requires addressing not only operational practices but also the socio-economic and systemic factors that underpin racial disparities, ensuring a holistic approach to reform. Reform efforts must address these intersecting concerns to strike a balance between effective crime prevention and the protection of civil liberties. Future initiatives, such as the UNJUST project, will be instrumental in providing evidence-based insights to guide policy reforms, ensuring that stop and search practices are equitable, transparent, and conducive to public trust.
